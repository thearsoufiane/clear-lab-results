TSH Normal But Still Tired? Here's What You're Missing

TSH is the most ordered thyroid test — and it's also the most incomplete picture of how your thyroid is actually working. Your doctor can look at TSH alone and declare you "fine," while you're still exhausted, cold, and struggling to lose weight.

The reason: TSH is a signal from your pituitary, not a direct measure of the thyroid hormones actually circulating in your blood. Here's why that gap matters — and what tests you need to fill it.

Plain-English guide · reviewed for accuracy · not a substitute for medical advice

What TSH actually measures

TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) is made by your pituitary gland, not your thyroid. It's a signal that tells your thyroid "make more hormones." When your actual thyroid hormones are high, TSH drops (negative feedback). When they're low, TSH rises to try to kick the thyroid into action.

The catch: TSH can stay "normal" even when your actual thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) are low or out of balance. Your pituitary is still saying "you're fine," but your cells aren't getting the hormones they need.

The three thyroid numbers that matter (and why doctors miss them)

A complete thyroid picture includes four things. Most labs only order one:

  • TSH — the signal from your pituitary. Normal range: ~0.4–4.0 mIU/L, but many clinicians note symptoms below 2.
  • Free T4 — the actual thyroid hormone available to your cells (the "free" part means not bound to proteins). Normal range varies widely by lab.
  • Free T3 — the active hormone your cells actually use. Critically important, rarely tested.
  • Thyroid antibodies — TPO and thyroglobulin antibodies detect autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto's / Graves'). Can explain why you feel bad even with "normal" hormones.

Why you can feel terrible with "normal" TSH

Hypothyroid symptoms show up even when TSH looks fine, because TSH is delayed in rising — your actual hormones can be falling for months before TSH reacts. Or, your Free T3 (the active hormone) is low while T4 is adequate — you're not converting efficiently. Or you have Hashimoto's antibodies silently attacking your thyroid.

The constellation of symptoms is familiar:

Fatigue Cold hands & feet Brain fog Weight gain Dry skin Hair shedding Constipation Low mood

What to ask your doctor

If your TSH is "normal" but you have symptoms, ask directly: "Can we test my Free T4, Free T3, and thyroid antibodies (TPO and thyroglobulin)?" Many doctors will happily do it once you ask. Some will resist, claiming it's unnecessary — but it's standard in functional medicine and thyroid-specific care.

Once you have those numbers:

  • Look for Free T3 in the upper half of the range (not just "in range").
  • Look for Free T4 similarly — upper half usually feels better than lower half.
  • Negative TPO antibodies is good; positive means autoimmune disease is present.

The conversion problem (T4 to T3)

Your thyroid makes mostly T4, which is inactive. Your body converts it to T3, the active hormone. If you're low in selenium, zinc, iron, or iodine — or if you're under chronic stress — this conversion fails. You can supplement T4 all day and still feel hypothyroid because your Free T3 stays low. This is why the full panel matters.
