Vitamin D Deficiency: What Low Levels Actually Mean

Vitamin D is the hormone that governs mood, bone strength, immune function, and inflammation. When levels are low, the effects ripple through your entire body — fatigue, depression, weak bones, and slow recovery from illness are the most common complaints.

Yet most people who feel these things get told their vitamin D is "normal." Here's what the number actually means — and why "normal" often misses the problem.

Plain-English guide · reviewed for accuracy · not a substitute for medical advice

What vitamin D actually does

Vitamin D isn't just about bone health (though that's part of it). It regulates immune function, mood, inflammation, and how your cells respond to stress. Most of your tissues have vitamin D receptors, so when levels fall short, the effects show up almost everywhere — mood takes a hit, infections linger longer, and general fatigue sets in.

The challenge: vitamin D status isn't obvious from the outside. People can have critically low levels and still look and act "fine" until symptoms pile up enough to notice. By then, the deficiency has usually been building for months.

"Normal" vs "optimal": the gap nobody talks about

This is the same issue as with ferritin and most other biomarkers. Lab reference ranges are set wide, and "normal" means "not yet a diagnosed disease" — not "optimal for feeling well." For vitamin D especially, the gap between lab-normal and clinically-optimal is huge.

Why you can feel terrible with "normal" vitamin D

Vitamin D deficiency doesn't announce itself loudly. Instead, it creeps up as a constellation of vague symptoms that most people chalk up to aging, stress, or just life:

Persistent fatigue Depression or low mood Muscle aches Slow wound healing Frequent infections Poor exercise tolerance Bone pain Impaired focus

If that list feels familiar and your bloodwork came back "normal," it's worth asking specifically for your vitamin D number — not just whether you're flagged as deficient.

Why so many people are low

  • Limited sun exposure — your skin makes vitamin D from UVB sunlight, which means winter, indoor work, and sunscreen all reduce production.
  • Geography — people above ~40° latitude (most of North America and Europe) can't produce meaningful vitamin D from the sun half the year.
  • Darker skin — higher melanin reduces vitamin D synthesis from sunlight (which is why deficiency is more common in people with darker skin, especially those at northern latitudes).
  • Diet — very few foods naturally contain vitamin D; it's mostly in fatty fish, egg yolks, and mushrooms exposed to sunlight.
  • Absorption problems — conditions like celiac disease or Crohn's can block vitamin D absorption even if intake is adequate.

What to ask your doctor

If your vitamin D is in the "normal" range but you have symptoms, a useful question is: "What's my actual number, and would you recommend supplementing?" Some clinicians will proactively suggest it; many wait for you to ask.

If you do supplement, a few things matter:

  • Form — vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is generally more effective than D2 (ergocalciferol).
  • Dose — typical supplementation ranges from 1,000–4,000 IU daily for maintenance; correcting a deficiency often needs more.
  • Retest — vitamin D takes 8–12 weeks to shift meaningfully, so retest after that window to see if you've corrected it.
  • Context — vitamin D works best with adequate calcium, magnesium, and K2, though the science on this is still evolving.

One important note: too much vitamin D is also a problem

While deficiency is far more common, it's possible to get too much vitamin D, especially from supplements. Very high levels (over 100 ng/mL) can cause toxicity. This is why supplementing makes sense only after testing, not preemptively — you want to know where you actually are.
