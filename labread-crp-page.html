What Does High CRP Mean? The Inflammation Marker Most People Miss

CRP (C-reactive protein) measures chronic inflammation — a quiet fire running in your body that predicts heart disease, stroke, diabetes, and autoimmune problems years before symptoms show. Yet most people have never heard of it, and most doctors don't order it.

That's a gap you need to know about. Here's what CRP actually measures, why it matters more than most markers, and what to do if it's elevated.

Plain-English guide · reviewed for accuracy · not a substitute for medical advice

What CRP measures (and why it's so useful)

CRP is an inflammatory protein your liver makes in response to inflammation anywhere in your body. Unlike blood sugar or cholesterol, CRP is agnostic — it rises when your body is inflamed, period. That inflammation could be from an infection, arthritis, a bad diet, chronic stress, poor sleep, obesity, or smoking.

What makes CRP powerful: it predicts future disease. Studies show that high CRP correlates with heart attack and stroke risk *independent of cholesterol*. It predicts diabetes, Alzheimer's, and even certain cancers. And most people have no idea their CRP is high because they never get tested.

The normal range (and what "normal" actually means)

Standard lab ranges say CRP under 3.0 mg/L is "normal." But that range was set including sick and healthy people — it's not optimal, it's just "not acutely inflamed." Many cardiologists and longevity-focused doctors argue for a lower threshold:

The nuance: CRP under 1.0 is what many cardiologists consider truly "optimal" for long-term health, though this threshold is still being debated. For now, the key is knowing your actual number and understanding what's driving it if it's elevated.

What causes high CRP (and what to do about it)

CRP rises with almost any inflammatory state. The common culprits:

  • Poor diet — ultra-processed foods, refined carbs, seed oils. The single biggest driver for many people.
  • Chronic stress and poor sleep — inflammation is your body's stress response.
  • Lack of movement — sedentary life drives inflammation; exercise reduces it.
  • Infection or autoimmune disease — CRP rises while your body fights something.
  • Obesity — fat tissue produces inflammatory cytokines.
  • Smoking — major inflammatory trigger.

The good news: CRP is one of the most responsive markers to lifestyle change. Diet, sleep, exercise, and stress management all lower it. Unlike genetics, you can actually move the needle here.

What to ask your doctor

Ask for "high-sensitivity CRP" (hs-CRP) — it's more precise than standard CRP. Many doctors will order it once you ask. If your CRP is elevated and you want to understand why, the conversation then shifts to ruling out acute infection (which your doctor will do) and addressing chronic lifestyle factors (which is mostly on you).

If your CRP is persistently elevated despite lifestyle changes, your doctor may investigate for underlying autoimmune or inflammatory conditions.

Why so few people know about CRP

CRP isn't part of standard bloodwork — you have to specifically request it. And most doctors don't routine order it because standard medicine focuses on acute illness, not prevention. You have to be the one who knows it matters.
